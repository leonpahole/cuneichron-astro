# Early Roman Empire

## Timeline

### Octavian consolidates power

- 29 BCE: Octavian celebrated three triumphs for his victories in Illyricum, Actium, and Egypt.
- ~28 BCE: Octavian and Agrippa reduced civil-warm armies, stationing some in frontiers and conducated a census to review Senate membership.
- ~28–25 BCE: Agrippa constructed the original Pantheon, while Augustus dedicated the Temple of Apollo on the Palatine Hill.
- 27 BCE: Octavian restored Senate authority but received 10 years of provincial control with most Roman legions and honorific title Augustus. He called himself princeps (first citizen), maintaining republican traditions while having authority.
- ~29–19 BCE: Virgil composed the Aeneid, connecting Rome's mythical origins and the Julian family to Aeneas, helping legitimize Augustus's rule.

### Augustus

Transformed the Republic into an imperial system while preserving the appearance of republican government. His reign established the Principate and began the Pax Romana.

- 27–19 BCE: Augustus pursued the conquest of northern Hispania (Cantabrian Wars).
- 25 BCE: Augustus arranged the marriage of his daughter Julia to his nephew Marcellus, whom he favored as a successor.
- 23 BCE: Augustus became ill, exposing the uncertainty of succession.
- 23 BCE: Augustus resigned consulship and received perpetual tribuneship + proconsular authority, allowing him control without monopolizing the consulship.
- 23 BCE: Marcellus died unexpectedly, disrupting Augustus's succession plans and increasing Agrippa's importance.
- 22 BCE: During a severe grain shortage, populace called for Augustus to become dictator but he refused, preserving the republican appearance.
- 21 BCE: Arranged the marriage of Julia to Marcus Agrippa, strengthening the succession plans.
- 20 BCE: Diplomatic agreement with Parthia, recovering Roman legionary standards.
- ~19–18 BCE: Reorganized the Senate (decision body) and established permanent provinical administrative staffs.
- ~20 BCE: Established more roads and relay stations to improve communication across the empire.
- 18 BCE: Introduced legislation encouraging marriage and childbearing, while imposing restrictions on unmarried and childless and banning adultery.
- 17 BCE: Adopted his grandsons Gaius and Lucius Caesar, intending to prepare them as future successors.
- 16 BCE: Augustus decided a good northern border would be on Danube and Elbe.
- ~16–15 BCE: Rome consolidated control over Noricum and Raetia, in preparation for extending the northern frontier toward the Danube.
- 12 BCE: Marcus Agrippa died, creating uncertainty over the imperial succession. Augustus arranged for Tiberius to divorce Vipsania and marry Julia.
- 12 BCE: Augustus became Pontifex Maximus following the death of Lepidus in 13 BCE, consolidating his religious authority.
- ~12–9 BCE: Tiberius led campaigns in Pannonia, extending Roman control toward the Danube.
- 12–9 BCE: Drusus led campaigns into Germania, reaching the Elbe River but failing to establish permanent control.
- 9 BCE: Drusus died following an accident during his Germanic campaign, leaving Tiberius as most likely potential successor.
- 8 BCE: Maecenas, Augustus's longtime political adviser and patron of Roman literature, died.
- 6 BCE: Tiberius unexpectedly withdrew from public life to Rhodes, possibly because of his unhappy marriage and uncertain succession.
- 2 BCE: Augustus exiled his daughter Julia following accusations of adultery.
- 1 BCE–4 CE: Gaius Caesar led a diplomatic and military mission in the East to secure Roman influence in Armenia and negotiate with Parthia.
- 2 CE: Lucius Caesar died unexpectedly from illness while traveling to Hispania.
- 4 CE: Gaius Caesar died following complications from a wound sustained during a campaign in Armenia.

### Transfer of power

- 4 CE: Augustus adopted stepson Tiberius as his heir, requiring him to adopt Germanicus.
- 7–8 CE: Augustus exiled his grandson Agrippa Postumus and granddaughter Julia the Younger, amid accusations of misconduct.
- ~7–9 CE: Rome expanded military presence east of the Rhine, establishing forts and alliances with Germanic communities.
- September 9 CE: Battle of the Teutoburg Forest: a coalition of Germanic warriors led by Arminius ambushed and destroyed three Roman legions.
- ~9–11 CE: Tiberius led campaigns to stabilize the Rhine frontier but Rome never established control over Germania east of the river.
- ~6 CE: Augustus established a treasury to finance veterans' retirement and reduce dependence on generals, and a permanent firefighting/night patrol service.
- ~1–14 CE: Augustus strengthened imperial administration, expanding the use of permanent officials and reforming provincial taxation to reduce corruption.
- ~9–14 CE: Following defeat in Germania, Augustus prioritized defending existing imperial territories rather than pursuing expansion.
- 14 CE: Augustus finalized an autobigraophy of his achievements, victories, spending, and honors.
- 19 August 14 CE: Augustus died at Nola, aged 75.
- August 14 CE: Agrippa Postumus, Augustus's exiled grandson and a potential rival to Tiberius, was executed shortly after Augustus's death.
- 14 CE: Tiberius succeeded Augustus as princeps. The Senate deified Augustus.

### Tiberius

An experienced general and capable administrator who consolidated Augustus's imperial system. His later reign became associated with treason trials, political repression, and withdrawal from public life.

- ~14–23 CE: Generally pursued a cautious foreign policy, avoiding major wars and emphasizing fiscal restraint and provincial administration.
- 14 CE: Legions in Pannonia and along the Rhine mutinied, demanding better benefits. Drusus the Younger (son of Tiberius) suppressed the Pannonian mutiny, Germanicus (nephew of Tiberius) supressed the Rhine one and rejected soldiers' suggestions that he challenge Tiberius.
- 15 CE: Lucius Aelius Sejanus became sole prefect of the Praetorian Guard.
- 15–16 CE: Germanicus led punitive campaigns across the Rhine against Germanic tribes, seeking revenge for Teutoburg Forest. He defeated forces led by Arminius several times but failed to establish lasting control over Germania.
- 17 CE: Out of fear of his popularity, Tiberius recalled Germanicus, threw him a triumph and appointed him authority over eastern provinces.
- 17–19 CE: Germanicus worked in eastern provinces to stabilize relations with client kingdoms, encountering opposition from Piso, governor of Syria.
- 19 CE: Germanicus visited Egypt without Tiberius's permission, violating restrictions on senatorial access.
- 10 October 19 CE: Germanicus died at Antioch following an illness. Suspicions of poisoning implicated Piso and, indirectly, Tiberius. Piso faced trial and died by suicide before the proceedings concluded, deepening public suspicion of Tiberius.
- ~15–23 CE: Sejanus became sole prefect of the Praetorian Guard, and concentrated them near Rome, strenghtening its political influence.
- 23 CE: Drusus died unexpectedly, possibly by poisoning from Sejanus and Drusus's wife Livilla.
- ~23–27 CE: Tiberius increasingly relied on Sejanus to manage affairs, while he eventually withdrew from Rome.
- 25-31 CE: Sejanus built power as Livia (mother of Tiberius) died, persecuting Agrippina and her sons and using accusations of treason to eliminate opponents and becoming consul. He was denied marrying Livilla, hoping to strengthen his position within the dynasty.
- 18 October 31 CE: Tiberius turned against Sejanus, arranging his arrest and execution.
- 33 CE: Agrippina and her son died in captivity, eliminating two prominent members of Germanicus's family from the imperial succession.
- ~31–37 CE: Tiberius intensified treason trials following Sejanus's downfall, persecuting political opponents; he remained on Capri.
- 35 CE: Tiberius named Caligula and his grandson Tiberius Gemellus as joint heirs.
- 16 March 37 CE: Tiberius died; accounts disagree whether naturally or murdered. Caligula succeded him with Praetorian support, executing Gemellus.

### Caligula

Initially popular, but remembered for increasingly autocratic rule, extravagant spending, and hostility toward the Senate.

- ~14–16 CE: Germanicus's young son Gaius earned the nickname Caligula ("Little Boots") from soldiers along the Rhine.
- ~31–37 CE: Caligula lived with Tiberius on Capri, surviving persecutions.
- 33 CE: Caligula received an honorary quaestorship.
- 37 CE: Caligula ended prosecutions, recalled exiles, reduced taxes, increased military pay, restored some client rulers, organized public games, earning popularity.
- October 37 CE: Caligula suffered a serious illness but recovered, possibly changing his behavior.
- 38 CE: Caligula's favorite sister Drusilla died and was deified.
- ~38–40 CE: Caligula's behavior turned: he routinely humiliated the Senate, persecuted aristocrats, confiscated property, spent extravagantly and promoted his own divinity; installation of his statue in Temple of Jerusalem provoked strong Jewish opposition.
- ~38–40 CE: Caligula sponsored public works, including improvements to grain ports and the construction of aqueducts.
- 39 CE: Caligula led meaningless expeditions to the Rhine frontier and next to English Channel.
- 39 CE: A conspiracy involving the governor of Upper Germania was uncovered, leading to executions and the exile of Caligula's sisters.
- 24 January 41 CE: Caligula was assassinated by Praetorian officers, his wife and infant daughter were subsequently killed.

### Claudius

An underestimated scholar who proved an effective administrator, expanding Roman territory, notably into Britain, and strengthening imperial bureaucracy. His reign was also marked by palace intrigue and succession struggles.

- ~10 BCE–37 CE: Claudius (brother of Germanicus) was excluded from political life due to his speech and physical difficulties but became a respected scholar of Etruscan and Carthaginian history.
- 37 CE: Caligula appointed his uncle Claudius as co-consul, giving him his first major political office.
- 41 CE: Following Caligula's assassination, the Praetorian Guard proclaimed Claudius emperor, forcing a divided Senate to accept his succession. Claudius rewarded the Guard to secure loyalty, and executed leading assassin of Caligula (clemency to others).
- 42 CE: A rebellion led by the governor of Dalmatia collapsed when his legions refused to support him.
- ~41–54 CE: Claudius took more active role in governing, strenghetning administration, expanding bureacracy, employing freedmen as advisers, and reforming.
- 43 CE: Claudius launched invasion of Britain under Plautius, partially to boost his legitimacy. Roman forces defeated resistance near Medway and Thames rivers; Claudius briefly visited and accepted the submission of several local rulers. Camulodunum (Colchester) became an important center.
- ~43–47 CE: Rome consolidated its conquest of southeastern Britain through administration, taxation, and veteran settlements; Vespasian led campaigns west.
- 44 CE: Britain became a Roman province.
- 50 CE: Rome defeated fugitive king Caratacus in western Britain; his life was spared after his public appearance in Rome.
- ~9–38 CE: Claudius's first and second (adoptive sister of Sejanus) ended in divorce, third was to Messalina, whose connections to Caligula helped protect Claudius.
- ~41–46 CE: Claudius annexed client territories, including Noricum, Lycia, Pamphylia, and Thrace.
- ~41–48 CE: Messalina and imperial freedmen, including Narcissus, used accusations of conspiracy to eliminate political rivals.
- 47 CE: At Secular Games, Agrippina the Younger and her son (Nero) received greater public acclaim than Messalina and Britannicus, highlighting competing succession interests.
- 48 CE: Messalina had numerous affairs and married the senator Silius as part of a scheme to replace the emperor. They were executed after being exposed.
- 47 CE: Claudius revived the censorship, removing political opponents and admitted provinicals from Gaul into the Senate.
- ~41–52 CE: Claudius undertook a project to drain Lake Fucinus and reclaim agricultural land, but it had limited success.
- ~41–44 CE: Rome suppressed resistance in Mauretania following Caligula's execution of its king, dividing the territory into two provinces.
- 41–48 CE: Claudius restored Herod Agrippa I's rule over Judaea (41–44), then returned it to direct Roman administration after his death, while granting other territories to his son Agrippa II.
- 49 CE: Claudius married his niece Agrippina, strengthening ties between the Julian and Claudian branches and bringing Nero into the family.
- 50 CE: Under Agrippina's influence, Claudius gave her title Augusta and adopted Nero, placing him ahead of Britannicus in the succession.
- 53 CE: Nero married Claudius's daughter Octavia, further securing his position within the imperial dynasty.
- 13 October 54 CE: Claudius died aged 63; ancient historians accused Agrippina of poisoning him, but the allegation remains unproven.

### Nero

Popular among ordinary Romans for his public games, entertainment, and generosity, but increasingly disliked by the aristocracy for his unconventional behavior and political repression.

- 13 October 54 CE: Praetorian Guard proclaimed 16-year-old Nero emperor, and the Senate confirmed his accession, bypassing Claudius's son Britannicus.
- 54 CE: Claudius was deified, while Agrippina gained considerable political influence as his widow and Nero's mother.
- ~54–55 CE: Nero initially governed under the influence of Agrippina, the philosopher Seneca, Praetorian prefect Burrus, and imperial freedman Pallas.
- 55 CE: Nero's relationship with a freedwoman intensified his conflict with Agrippina, while Seneca and Burrus increasingly displaced her as his advisers.
- 55 CE: Agrippina threatened to support Britannicus's claim to the throne; Britannicus died suddenly, allegedly poisoned on Nero's orders.
- ~55–57 CE: Nero dismissed Pallas and increasingly excluded Agrippina from government, eventually forcing her to leave the imperial palace.
- ~54–59 CE: Nero's enthusiasm for music, chariot racing, and theatrical performance challenged traditional Roman aristocratic expectations of conduct.
- 59 CE: Nero arranged the murder of Agrippina.
- ~55–57 CE: Nero appointed Corbulo to prepare a campaign against Parthian influence in Armenia after negotiations failed.
- 58-60 CE: Corbulo invaded Armenia, forced Tiridates to abandon his stronholds and installed the Roman-backed Tigranes VI as king.
- 61-62 CE: Tigranes VI raided Parthian territory, provoking Vologases I to intervene; Roman commander Paetus suffered a humiliating defeat.
- 63 CE: Corbulo negotiated a treaty allowing Parthia to nominate Armenia's king but it had to be approven by Roman emperor; Tiridates was crowned by Nero.
- 60–61 CE: Following the death of Iceni king, Roman authorities refused to accept his will to his widow Boudica and annexed his kingdom, provoking rebelion.
- 60–61 CE: Boudica's forces destroyed several settlements: Camulodunum (Colchester), Londinium (London) and Verulamium (St Albans).
- 60–61 CE: Suetonius Paulinus defeated Boudica's larger army at the Battle of Watling Street, ending the revolt.
- 62 CE: Nero divorced his popular wife Claudia Octavia, married Poppaea Sabina, and ordered Octavia's execution following public protests.
- 62 CE: Praetorian prefect Burrus died, Seneca withdrew from politics, and Tigellinus gained influence, weakening moderating influence of Nero's advisers.
- 64 CE: The Great Fire of Rome devastated much of the city; Nero organized relief but was suspected of starting the fire as he later built Domus Aurea on land cleared partly by the fire. Christians were blamed and prosecuted (St. Peter); cruelty provoked sympathy even among non-Christians.
- 65 CE: The Pisonian conspiracy, involving prominent Romans, attempted to overthrow Nero but was exposed; Nero ordered executions, including Seneca.
