# Late Roman republic

Rome's Mediterranean conquests brought wealth, slaves, and opportunities for political advancement, but these benefits were distributed unevenly. Aristocrats established personal patronage networks abroad and competed for lucrative military commands and provincial appointments.

In Italy, wealthy landowners consolidated agricultural estates, often employing slaves. Small farmers faced pressures, and some migrated to cities for work. As fewer citizens met the property qualifications for recruitment, they were gradually lowered. Armies became longer-serving and soldiers depended on commanders for rewards and post-service settlement. Individual generals were given more authority.

Tensions between the ruling elite and poorer citizens intensified. Because aristocrats died during the Second Punic War, more _novus homo_ entered politics, and they had no interest in upkeeping existing agreements between high-class. They became populist, leading to split of populares and optimates.

The Gracchi’s reforms and the Social War exposed disputes over who shared in Rome’s gains. As rival leaders used armies to settle political disputes, civil wars concentrated power first in Sulla and Caesar, then in Octavian, whose victory over Antony and Cleopatra left him without a military rival.

### Tiberius Gracchus

- 146 BCE: Served under Scipio Aemilianus during the destruction of Carthage. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[10.323,36.853]}`
- 137 BCE: Negotiated a treaty in Numantia to save a trapped army. The Senate rejected it, damaging his standing and turning him to populism. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[-3.0,40.0]}`
- 133 BCE: As tribune, proposed land cap and redistribution; removed a Senate-backed tribune that vetoed it and bypassed the Senate to pass it. - 133 BCE: Sought re-election to avoid political prosecution, provoking accusations of single rule; a group led by senators killed him and many supporters. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`

### Gaius Gracchus

- 154-133 BCE: Early life, mother Cornelia raised him, inherited political networks and expectations from his brother. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 126–124 BCE: Served as quaestor in Sardinia, then returned to Rome without authorization and won election as tribune. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[9.0,40.0]}`
- 123 BCE: Subsidized grain, revived land redistribution, restricted capital punishment without popular approval, barred recruitment under 17, provided soldiers with state-funded clothing, and transferred extortion court juries from senators to equestrians. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 122 BCE: Second tribunate: reformed provincial taxation and promoted colonies to ease land pressure, overseeing preparations at Carthage. His proposal to extend citizenship rights to Italian allies failed, and he lost his bid for a third term. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.5,42.5]}`
- 121 BCE: After a supporter killed a consul’s attendant, the Senate issued an emergency decree. Killed among with many supported in the ensuing crackdown. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`

### Marius

- 157 BCE: Born near Arpinum in Latium, into a locally prominent family. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[13.61,41.64]}`
- 134–133 BCE: Served under Scipio Aemilianus during the Numantine War, gaining experience and recognition. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[-2.43,41.8]}`
- 119–~110 BCE: As tribune, curbed interference with voters. Later won the praetorship as a novus homo, governed Further Spain, and married Julia of the prominent Julii Caesares. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 109–107 BCE: Served under Metellus in the Jugurthine War, won consulship by promising a quick victory, then recruited volunteers below the traditional property threshold. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[8.0,34.0]}`
- 106–105 BCE: His quaestor Sulla persuaded the king of Mauretania to hand over Jugurtha. The capture ended the war and fueled rivalry over credit. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[6.6,36.36]}`
- 109–104 BCE: The Cimbri and their allies repeatedly defeated Roman armies, culminating at Arausio (consul dispute) in 105 BCE. Their move into Hispania gave Marius time to rebuild and train Rome’s forces. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[2.0,46.0]}`
- ~104–100 BCE: Adopted cohort as main tactical unit, standardized equpiment, recruited poor, carrying their own equipment and intensified training. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 102–101 BCE: Defeated the Teutones and Ambrones at Aquae Sextiae, then the Cimbri at Vercellae, ending the threat to Italy. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[5.447,43.529]}`
- 100 BCE: During sixth consulship, suppressed political unrest and alienated former allies in the popular movement. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`

### Social war

- 91 BCE: Italian allies demanded citizenship as they contributed to Rome's campaigns; after the tribune that proposed citizenship was assasinated, several allies (including Samnites) rebelled. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.5,42.5]}`
- 90–89 BCE: Early Roman defeats brought Marius back to command. Sulla defeated southern rebels, while Pompeius Strabo captured Asculum. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 90–89 BCE: Rome offered citizenship to loyal allies and rebels who accepted Roman authority, weakening the revolt and bringing most Italian communities into the citizenship. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`

### Sulla - First Mithridatic War

- 88 BCE: Mithridates VI of Pontus invaded Roman Asia Minor and ordered the Asiatic Vespers massacre. A dispite over command of the war led Sulla to march his army on Rome, Marius fled to exile. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 87 BCE: While Sulla campaigned in Greece, the ousted consul Cinna joined Marius to retake Rome and carry out reprisals. Marius died early in his seventh consulship, leaving Cinna dominant. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 86 BCE: Sulla captured and sacked Athens, then defeated Mithridates at Chaeronea and Orchomenus, securing control of mainland Greece. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[22.0,39.0]}`
- 86–85 BCE: A rival Roman army dispatched east, mutinied and continued fighting Mithridates independently of Sulla. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[12.4964,41.9028]}`
- 85–84 BCE: The Peace of Dardanus forced Mithridates to relinquish his conquests while retaining Pontus. Sulla imposed heavy financial penalties on cities in Roman Asia. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[36.5,40.5]}`

### Sulla the dictator

- 84–83 BCE: Cinna was killed in a mutiny while preparing to confront Sulla, who returned to Italy the following year to overthrow the Marian government.
- 83–82 BCE: Backed by Pompey, from a prominent Italian military and political family, the wealthy Roman aristocrat Crassus, and Metellus Pius, Sulla defeated the younger Marius at Sacriportus and besieged him at Praeneste. Crassus helped secure Rome at the Colline Gate against Samnite-led army.
- 82–81 BCE: Appointed dictator with no fixed term, Sulla proscribed political enemies and confiscated their property, enriching supporters such as Crassus.
- 82–81 BCE: Pompey defeated remaining Marian forces in Sicily and Africa and celebrated a triumph despite never having held a magistracy.
- 81–80 BCE: Sulla expanded the Senate, curtailed the tribunes’ powers, tightened the rules for holding magistracies, restored senatorial control of juries, settled veterans on confiscated land and reorganized the cursus honorum.
- ~80–78 BCE: Sulla voluntarily gave up the dictatorship, retired from public life, and died in 78 BCE.
- 78–72 BCE: Pompey helped defeat a revolt against Sulla’s settlement, then received an exceptional command in Hispania against Marian commander Sertorius. Resistance collapsed after Sertorius was assassinated in 72 BCE.

### Servile Wars

- 135–132 BCE: First Servile war: enslaved people in Sicily revolted against harsh treatment by wealthy landowners; suppressed by Rome.
- 104–100 BCE: Second Servile war: sparked over a dispute over the enslavement of Rome’s allies; suppressed by Rome.
- 73 BCE: Spartacus and Crixus led ~70 gladiators in an escape from Capua, starting the Third Servile War. From a base near Vesuvius, they recruited enslaved people and defeated several Roman forces.
- 72 BCE: The rebels split. Crixus stayed in southern Italy and was killed, while Spartacus marched north, defeated Roman consular armies, then turned south for unclear reasons.
- 72–71 BCE: Crassus took command and defeated Spartacus after his attempt to cross to Sicily failed. Crassus had ~6,000 captives crucified along the Appian Way. Pompey intercepted fleeing rebels and claimed credit for ending the war.
- 70 BCE: Crassus and Pompey became consuls together despite their rivalry.

### Third Mithridatic War and Pompey's conquests

- 74–70 BCE: Rome inherited Bithynia from Nicomedes IV. Mithridates VI invaded it, but commander Lucullus drove him out and conquered much of Pontus, forcing him to seek refuge with Tigranes II in Armenia.
- 69–67 BCE: Lucullus defeated Tigranes at Tigranocerta, but his campaign in Armenia stalled and he lost political support in Rome.
- 67–66 BCE: Senate gave Pompey sweeping powers to suppress Mediterranean piracy. After defeating and resettling pirates, he took command against Mithridates.
- 66 BCE: Pompey defeated Mithridates in Pontus, accepted Tigranes’ submission, and made Armenia a Roman client kingdom.
- 65 BCE: Pompey campaigned in the Caucasus against local kingdoms, extending Roman military influence beyond Anatolia.
- 64–63 BCE: Pompey annexed the remaining Seleucid territories in Syria, then intervened in a Judean succession dispute and captured Jerusalem, bringing Judea under Roman dominance.
- 63 BCE: Mithridates died after losing his remaining political and military support, ending the war.
- 61 BCE: Pompey celebrated a triumph in Rome after expanding Roman control in the east and reportedly doubling Roman tax revenues.

### Caesar - Early career

- 100 BCE: Born into the patrician Julii, an old family whose political influence had declined.
- ~87–81 BCE: Married Cinna’s daughter Cornelia and was nominated as priest of Jupiter. He went into hiding after refusing Sulla’s order to divorce her.
- ~80–75 BCE: Earned the civic crown for bravery at Mytilene. Later, pirates captured him en route to Rhodes; he captured and executed them after his release.
- 69 BCE: Served as quaestor in Further Spain and publicly displayed images of Marius at his aunt Julia’s funeral, helping restore Marius’s reputation.
- 65–62 BCE: Gained popularity as aedile through lavish games and building projects that left him in debt. Elected chief priest in 63 BCE, he served as praetor in 62 BCE, siding with Pompey while maintaining financial ties to Crassus.

### Caitiline conspiracy

- ~80s BCE: Cicero established himself as a prominent lawyer and orator, combining Roman legal practice with Greek philosophy and rhetoric.
- 64 BCE: Cicero defeated Catiline in the consular elections, benefiting from senatorial opposition to Catiline's proposals for debt relief.
- 63 BCE: After losing another election, Catiline organized a conspiracy. Cicero denounced him before the Senate, and Catiline fled to join an armed revolt in Etruria. Evidence from Gallic envoys led to arrests and executions in Rome.
  62 BCE: Roman forces defeated Catiline’s army at Pistoria, killing him and ending the revolt.

### Caesar - Rise

- 61 BCE: Successful campaigns as governor of Further Spain brought Caesar wealth and military prestige. He forwent a triumph to stand for consul.
- 60 BCE: Won election as consul and entered an informal political alliance with Pompey and Crassus (First Triumvirate).
- 59 BCE: As consul, he overcame Bibulus’s opposition to settle Pompey’s veterans, ratify Pompey’s eastern settlements, and tighten rules against provincial corruption. Pompey married Caesar’s daughter Julia, strengthening their alliance.
- 59 BCE: Caesar enabled Clodius to seek the tribunate through adoption into a plebeian family, weakening Cicero’s position.
- 59 BCE: Secured a five-year command over Illyricum and Cisalpine and Transalpine Gaul, gaining military opportunities and protection from prosecution.

### Caesar - The Gallic wars

- 58 BCE: Caesar blocked the Helvetii (Celts) at the Rhône, defeating them at the Arar and Bibracte after they took another route and Gallic allies asked for help. Survivors had to return to their homeland, serving as a buffer against Germans.
- 58 BCE: Caesar defeated Ariovistus, a Germanic ruler that was expanding into Gaul, at Vosges, forcing retreat across the Rhine. Caesar stationed his legions in winter quarters within independent Gaul. `geo:{"type":"Point","coordinates":[6.5,48.0]}`
- 57-54 BCE: Defeated northern Belgic tribes, Veneti (maritime people of Brittany) and bridged the Rhine for a brief expedition into Germania.
- 55-54 BCE: After first expedition to Britain withdrew, he returned with a larger army, defeating rebels and securing agreements with British rulers.
- 54 BCE: Julia died in childbirth, weakening his relationship with Pompey.
- 54–53 BCE: Gallic rebels destroyed one Roman force and besieged another. Caesar relieved the siege, retaliated, and crossed the Rhine again to deter intervention.
- 52 BCE: Vercingetorix united several Gallic tribes and used scorched-earth tactics. Caesar captured Avaricum, lost at Gergovia, then forced Vercingetorix to surrender at Alesia after building two rings of siege fortifications.

### Set up to civil war

- 58 BCE: As tribune, Clodius drove Cicero into exile, introduced free grain distributions, and sent Cato to oversee the annexation of Cyprus.
- 57 BCE: Cicero returned as street violence between supporters of Clodius and Milo intensified.
- 55 BCE: Co-consuls Pompey and Crassus extended Caesar's command in Gaul and secured commands of Hispania (Pompey) and Syria (Crassus).
- 54–53 BCE: Crassus invaded Parthia and was killed with his son Publius at Carrhae. His death and Julia’s death dissolved the First Triumvirate.
- 52 BCE: Clodius was killed; his supporters burned the Senate house during his funeral; the Senate appointed Pompey sole consul to restore order.
- ~52–50 BCE: Pompey sided with Caesar’s opponents as disputes grew over when Caesar would surrender his command and whether he could stand for consul without risking prosecution. Caesar proposed that both men give up their commands, but negotiations failed.

### Civil war

- January 49 BCE: Tribunes including Mark Antony opposed the senate and fled to Caesar's camp.
- 10 January 49 BCE: Caesar crossed the Rubicon with Legio XIII.
- January–March 49 BCE: Caesar encountered limited resistance and showed clemency. Pompey and many senators withdrew to Greece via Brundisium.
- April–December 49 BCE: Caesar entered Rome and seized funds from the treasury, forced Pompey’s legions in Hispania to surrender, and briefly assumed the dictatorship to hold elections that secured his consulship.
- January-March 48 BCE: Caesar surprisingly crossed the Adriatic, and Antony brought reinforcements through the blockade.
- ~July 48 BCE: Battle of Dyrrhachium - Pompey broke through Caesar's fortifications and defeated his forces, but failed to pursue out of fear.
- 9 August 48 BCE: Battle of Pharsalus - Caesar decisively defeated Pompey's numerically superior army.
- August–September 48 BCE: Caesar pardoned several defeated opponents, including Brutus, continuing his policy of clemency.
- September 48–47 BCE: Ptolemy XIII’s government had Pompey killed on arrival in Egypt.
- 48–47 BCE: Alexandrian War - Caesar intervened in the Egyptian succession dispute, fought in Alexandria, and defeated Ptolemy at the Battle of the Nile, restoring Cleopatra VII under Roman protection.
- 47 BCE: Caesar defeated Pharnaces II at Zela (veni, vidi, vici).
- 47 BCE: Back in Rome, Caesar removed Antony from control of the city after unrest and persuaded mutinous veterans to return to service.
- 46 BCE: Caesar defeated the senatorial forces at Thapsus, and Cato took his own life at Utica. Back in Rome, Caesar celebrated triumphs (Gaul, Egypt, Pontus, and Africa); Vercingetorix was executed.
- ~46–44 BCE: Caesar reduced the grain rolls, founded overseas colonies for veterans and poorer citizens, extended citizenship to selected provincial communities, expanded the Senate and admitted provincials, undertook public works, and introduced the Julian calendar.
- 46–45 BCE: Caesar campaigned against Pompey’s sons in Hispania and narrowly defeated the last major Pompeian army at Munda.

### Caesar's dictatorship and assassination

- 46 BCE: Cleopatra VII visited Rome with her son Caesarion, strengthening ties between Rome and Egypt.
- 45 BCE: Caesar celebrated a triumph after his victory in Hispania, appointed loyalists as replacement consuls, and fuelled fears of monarchy by wearing triumphal regalia and accepting exceptional honours.
- Early 44 BCE: Caesar became dictator for life and prepared a campaign against Parthia. He publicly refused a diadem offered by Mark Antony.
- 15 March 44 BCE: A group of senators led by Brutus and Cassius assassinated Caesar during a Senate meeting, hoping to prevent monarchy. His death triggered a new political crisis.

### Octavian

- March 44 BCE: Mark Antony emerged as the leading Caesarian politician and reached a compromise with the Senate granting the assassins amnesty while preserving Caesar’s legislation. Unrest after Caesar’s funeral helped drive the assassins from Rome.
- Spring–autumn 44 BCE: Caesar’s will named his 18-year-old great-nephew Gaius Octavius as his adopted son and principal heir. Octavian returned from Apollonia, accepted the inheritance, and began competing with Antony for the loyalty of Caesar’s veterans.
- September–November 44 BCE: Cicero attacked Antony in the Philippics and backed Octavian, whose military position improved when two of Antony’s legions defected to him.
- Late 44–April 43 BCE: Antony marched to seize Cisalpine Gaul from Decimus Brutus. The Senate backed Brutus and granted Octavian command to join the consuls against Antony. They defeated him at Forum Gallorum and Mutina, but both consuls died, leaving Octavian in command.
- Summer 43 BCE: Antony joined Lepidus in Gaul, while Decimus Brutus lost his army and was executed.
- 19 August 43 BCE: Senate refused Octavian's demand for consulship; he marched on Rome and secured election as consul.

### Second triumvirate

- November–December 43 BCE: Octavian, Antony, and Lepidus formed the Second Triumvirate with sweeping legal powers for five years. They revived proscriptions and confiscated property to fund their armies. Cicero was among those executed.
- October 42 BCE: Antony and Octavian defeated Caesar’s assassins in two battles at Philippi. Cassius and Brutus committed suice.
- 42–40 BCE: Octavian confiscated Italian land to settle veterans, provoking a revolt led by Antony’s brother and wife. After defeating them, Octavian and Antony reconciled at Brundisium, dividing the west, east, and Africa between Octavian, Antony, and Lepidus. Antony married Octavian’s sister Octavia.
- 41 BCE: Antony secured Cleopatra’s support after meeting her in Cilicia.
- 40–38 BCE: Parthian forces invaded Roman Syria and Judea, but Antony's general defeated them.
- 42–36 BCE: Sextus Pompey used his fleet to disrupt Italy’s grain supply. The Pact of Misenum briefly lifted the blockade, but war resumed. After the triumvirate was renewed at Tarentum in 37 BCE, Antony supplied ships to Octavian, and Agrippa defeated Sextus’s fleet in 36 BCE. Lepidus tried to claim Sicily but lost power when his troops defected to Octavian.
- 38 BCE: Octavian married Livia Drusilla, whose descendants would become central to the Julio-Claudian dynasty.
- 36–34 BCE: Antony’s invasion of Media Atropatene ended in a costly retreat. He later seized Armenia and, in the Donations of Alexandria, granted royal titles and territorial claims to his children with Cleopatra. Caesarion’s recognition as Caesar’s son and Egypt’s co-ruler challenged Octavian's legitimacy.
- 35–33 BCE: Octavian campaigned in Illyricum, subduing rebellious communities and strengthening his military reputation.
- 33 BCE: The triumvirate expired; Octavian publicised Antony’s wish to be buried in Alexandria to discredit him. Antony’s supporters fled Rome.
- ~34–32 BCE: Octavian intensified propaganda against Antony, portraying his relationship with Cleopatra and adoption of eastern royal customs as a betrayal of Roman traditions; Rome declared war on Cleopatra.
- 2 September 31 BCE: After disrupting enemy supply lines, Agrippa and Octavian defeated Antony and Cleopatra’s fleet at Actium. The pair fled to Egypt, and their remaining land forces surrendered.
- August 30 BCE: Octavian took Alexandria. Antony and Cleopatra died shortly afterward, and Octavian had Caesarion executed and annexed Egypt, ending Ptolemaic rule.
