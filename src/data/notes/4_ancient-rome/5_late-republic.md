# Late Roman republic

Rome's Mediterranean conquests brought wealth, slaves, and opportunities for political advancement, but these benefits were distributed unevenly. Aristocrats established personal patronage networks abroad and competed for lucrative military commands and provincial appointments.

In Italy, wealthy landowners consolidated agricultural estates, often employing slaves. Small farmers faced pressures, and some migrated to cities for work. As fewer citizens met the property qualifications for recruitment, they were gradually lowered. Armies became longer-serving and soldiers depended on commanders for rewards and post-service settlement. Individual generals were given more authority.

Tensions between the ruling elite and poorer citizens intensified. Because aristocrats died during the Second Punic War, more _novus homo_ entered politics, and they had no interest in upkeeping existing agreements between high-class. They became populist, leading to split of populares and optimates.

## Timeline

### Tiberius Gracchus

- 168 BCE: Born into a prominent Roman family (mother Cornelia (mother of the Gracchi) was daughter of Scipio Africanus).
- 146 BCE: Served under Scipio Aemilianus during the destruction of Carthage.
- 137 BCE: In Hispania negotiated a treaty to save a trapped army; Senate rejected it, damaging his political standing and turning him to populism.
- 133 BCE: Elected tribune and proposed land cap and redistribution; a Senate-backed tribune vetoed the proposal and Tiberius removed him, challenging traditional tribune protections. He then bypassed Senate to pass the law through popular assembly.
- 133 BCE: A three-member commission began surveying and redistributing public land. Tiberius proposed using land King Attalus III of Pergamum willed to Rome to finance the reforms.
- 133 BCE: Sought re-election to avoid political prosecution after his term, which provoked accusations of personal rule. Killed along with many supporters by a group of senators during an electoral assembly.

### Gaius Gracchus

- 154-133 BCE: Early life, Cornelia (mother of the Gracchi) raised him, inherited political networks and expectations from his brother.
- 126 BCE: Served as quaestor in Sardinia; repeated extensions of his commander's appointment kept him away from Rome.
- 124 BCE: Returned to Rome without authorization and won election as tribune.
- 123 BCE: Reforms: selling grain at a subsidized price to address rising prices, restricting capital punishment without popular authorization, revived agrarian reforms, military (restrictions on recruiting under 17 and state-provided clothing), courts (jury from senators to equestrians).
- 122 BCE: Second tribunate: proposed extending Roman citizenship to Italian allies (not passed; faced opposition from senators and population), establishment of new Colonies (including in former Carthage) to reduce land pressure, changes to provincial taxation.
- 122 BCE: Travelled to Carthage to oversee colony preparations; his opponents gained influence during his absence. Failed to secure third tribunate.
- 121 BCE: Tensions escalated after his supporter killed an attendant of a consul; Senate issued an emergency decree urging the consuls to protect the Republic, giving consults justification to use armed action. Gaius and many supporters died during the violent suppression of his movement.

### Marius

- 157 BCE: Born near Arpinum in Latium, into a locally prominent family.
- 134–133 BCE: Served under Scipio Aemilianus during the Numantine War, gaining experience and recognition.
- 119 BCE: Elected tribune; reforms restricting interference with voters.
- 115 BCE: Elected praetor, advancing his career despite his status as a novus homo.
- 115 BCE: Crassus born into a wealthy Roman aristocratic family.
- ~114–113 BCE: Governed Further Spain, conducting military operations against local resistance.
- ~110 BCE: Married Julia, a member of the prominent Julii Caesares family.
- 109 BCE: Accompanied Metellus to Numidia during the Jugurthine War and won consulship by criticizing the slow campaign, promising a quick victory.
- 107 BCE: Recruited volunteers without traditional property qualifications.
- 106 BCE: His quaestor Sulla negotiated with king of Mauretania to secure betraying Jugurtha.
- 105 BCE: Jugurtha surrendered to Sulla, ending the war and beginning the rivalry between Marius and Sulla.
- 109-107 BCE: The Cimbri and Tigurini defeated Roman armies near southern Gaul multiple times.
- 106 BCE: Pompey born into a prominent Italian family with a strong military and political background.
- 105 BCE: The Cimbri and their allies annihilated Roman armies at Arausio, partly due to disputes between two consults.
- ~104–103 BCE: The Cimbri moved into Hispania while the Teutones remained in Gaul, giving Rome time to rebuild its forces under Marius.
- ~104–100 BCE: Adopted cohort as main tactical unit, standardized equpiment, recruited poor, carrying their own equipment and intensified training.
- 102 BCE: Defeated the Teutones and Ambrones at Aquae Sextiae.
- 101 BCE: Defeated the Cimbri who crossed the Alps at the Battle of Vercellae, ending the immediate threat.
- 100 BCE: Serving his sixth consulship, damaged his relationship with popular movement by suppressing political unrest.

### Social war

- ~125–91 BCE: Italian allies demanded citizenship as they contributed to Rome's military campaigns without enjoying equal rights.
- 91 BCE: A tribune proposed extending citizenship to Italian allies, but was assasinated, triggering several Italian peoples (including Samnites) to rebel and establish an independent confederation with capital at Corfinium.
- 90 BCE: Rome suffered several military defeats; Marius returned to military command.
- 90–89 BCE: Rome passed laws extending citizenship to loyal allies and rebels willing to accept Roman authority, gradually undermining the rebellion.
- 89 BCE: Sulla achieved major victories against the southern rebels, while Pompeius Strabo captured the stronghold of Asculum.
- ~89–87 BCE: The war ended with most Italian communities gaining Roman citizenship.

### Sulla - First Mithridatic War

- 88 BCE: Mithridates VI of Pontus invaded Roman-controlled territories in Asia Minor and orchestrated the Asiatic Vespers. A struggle over command of the war led Sulla to march his army on Rome, Marius fled to exile.
- 87 BCE: Sulla departed to fight Mithridates VI, while political conflict resumed between the consuls Cinna and Gnaeus Octavius. Cinna was expelled and allied with returning Marius; their forces captured the city and carried out violent reprisals.
- 86 BCE: Marius began his seventh consulship alongside Cinna but died shortly afterward, leaving Cinna as the dominant figure.
- 86 BCE: Sulla captured and sacked Athens after a prolonged siege, then defeated Mithridates VI at Chaeronea and Orchomenus, securing control of mainland Greece.
- 86–85 BCE: Rome dispatched a rival army to the east; after a mutiny it fought Mithridates VI independently.
- 85 BCE: Sulla concluded the Peace of Dardanus with Mithridates VI, requiring him to abandon his conquests while retaining kingdom of Pontus.
- 85–84 BCE: Sulla imposed heavy financial penalties on cities in Roman Asia.

### Sulla the dictator

- 84 BCE: Cinna killed during a military mutiny while preparing forces to confront Sulla.
- 83 BCE: Sulla returned to Italy to overthrow the Marian government, many commanders defected to his side.
- 83–82 BCE: Pompey, Crassus, and Metellus Pius supported Sulla, helping him defeat the Marian forces.
- 82 BCE: Sulla defeated the son of Marius at Sacriportus and besieged him at Praeneste, while his allies defeated other Marian armies across Italy.
- 82 BCE: Sulla defeated a Samnite-led army at the Battle of the Colline Gate, securing control of the capital; Crassus playing a decisive part in victory.
- 82 BCE: Sulla was appointed dictator with extraordinary powers and no fixed term limit.
- 82–81 BCE: Sulla introduced the proscriptions: lists of political enemies, property was confiscated, enriching his supporters.
- 82–81 BCE: Pompey defeated remaining Marian forces in Sicily and Africa, consolidating Sulla's control.
- 82–78 BCE: Crassus accumulated enormous wealth by purchasing confiscated property and investing in land, mining, and slaves.
- 81-80 BCE: Sulla expanded the Senate, restricted tribunal power, reorganized the cursus honorum, transferred jury control to senators and settled his veterans on confiscated Italian land.
- 81 BCE: Pompey celebrated a triumph for his victories in Africa despite never having held a magistracy, bypassing traditions.
- 81 BCE: Sulla proscribed Caesar as he refused to divorce daughter of Cinna Cornelia (daughter of Cinna), briefly going into hiding.
- ~80–79 BCE: Sulla voluntarily resigned his dictatorship and returned to ordinary political life, eventually retiring from public office.
- 78 BCE: Sulla died.
- 78–77 BCE: A consul attempted to challenge established order but was defeated by forces loyal to the Senate, including Pompey.
- 77 BCE: Pompey received proconsular command in Hispania despite lacking qualifications, campaigning against the Marian commander Sertorius.
- 77–72 BCE: Sertorius successfully resisted Roman armies in Hispania.
- 72 BCE: Sertorius assassinated, Pompey ends major resistance in Hispania.

### Servile Wars

- 135–132 BCE: The First Servile War (Sicily); slaves rebelled against harsh treatment by wealthy landowners; suppressed by Rome.
- 104–100 BCE: The Second Servile War (Sicily), due to disputes over the enslavement from Rome's allied communities; suppressed by Rome.
- 73 BCE: ~70 gladiators, led by Spartacus and Crixus, escaped from a gladiatorial school in Capua, beginning the Third Servile War. They established a base near Vesuvius, attracted slaves and defeated several Roman forces sent to suppress them.
- 72 BCE: The rebel army divided, with Crixus remaining in southern Italy while Spartacus marched north. Crixus was defeated and killed by Rome. Spartacus defeated Roman consular armies but then turned south for uncertain reasons.
- 72–71 BCE: Crassus received command against Spartacus and imposed strict military discipline.
- 71 BCE: Spartacus attempted to cross into Sicily but was abandoned by pirates. Crassus trapped his army and defeated them. Pompey intercepted and destroyed a separate group of fleeing rebels, claiming he won the war.
- 71 BCE: Crassus ordered ~6,000 captured rebels crucified along the Appian Way as a warning against future uprisings.
- 70 BCE: Crassus and Pompey became consuls together despite their rivalry.

### Third Mithridatic War and Pompey's conquests

- 74 BCE: King Nicomedes IV of Bithynia died and bequeathed his kingdom to Rome, provoking conflict with Mithridates VI of Pontus.
- 73–72 BCE: Mithridates invaded Bithynia. Roman commander Lucullus defeated his forces and invaded Pontus.
- 71–70 BCE: Lucullus conquered much of Pontus, forcing Mithridates to flee to Armenia, where he sought protection from son-in-law King Tigranes II.
- 69 BCE: Lucullus invaded Armenia and defeated Tigranes II at the Battle of Tigranocerta.
- 68–67 BCE: Lucullus's campaigns stalled amid difficult conditions, weakening his political support in Rome.
- 67 BCE: The Lex Gabinia granted Pompey authority across the Mediterranean to combat piracy; his naval campaign defeated and resettled pirates.
- 66 BCE: The Lex Manilia transferred command of the war against Mithridates to Pompey.
- 66 BCE: Pompey defeated Mithridates in Pontus; Tigranes II submitted to Rome, Armenia became a client kingdom.
- 66–65 BCE: Pompey campaigned in the Caucasus against local kingdoms, extending Roman military influence beyond Anatolia.
- 64 BCE: Pompey annexed remaining Seleucid territories in Syria.
- 63 BCE: Pompey intervened in dynastic conflict in Judea, captured Jerusalem, and established Roman dominance over the kingdom.
- 63 BCE: Mithridates VI died after losing his remaining political and military support.
- 61 BCE: Pompey returned to Rome and celebrated a triumph, having expanded Roman influence and doubled tax revenues.

### Caesar - Early career

- 100 BCE: Born into the ancient patrician Julii family, which had lost much of its former political prominence.
- ~87–82 BCE: Married Cornelia (daughter of Cinna), daughter of Cinna, and was nominated as Flamen Dialis (priest of Jupiter).
- 81 BCE: Went into hiding after Sulla stripped his priesthood and proscribed him as he refused to divorce Cornelia (daughter of Cinna).
- 80 BCE: Served in army in Asia Minor and received the corona civica for bravery during the siege of Mytilene.
- ~75 BCE: Captured by pirates while travelling to Rhodes, later capturing and executing them.
- 69 BCE: Served as quaestor in Hispania Ulterior. At his aunt Julia's funeral, he publicly displayed images of her husband Marius, helping rehabilitiate his image.
- 65 BCE: As aedile, financed lavish public games and building projects, gaining popularity while accumulating debts.
- 63 BCE: Elected Pontifex Maximus, Rome's chief priest.
- 62 BCE: Served as praetor, aligning himself with Pompey's while maintaining financial ties with Crassus.

### Caitiline conspiracy

- ~80s BCE: Cicero established himself as a prominent lawyer and orator, combining Roman legal practice with Greek philosophy and rhetoric.
- 64 BCE: Cicero defeated Catiline in the consular elections, benefiting from senatorial opposition to Catiline's proposals for debt relief.
- 63 BCE: Catiline lost another consular election and organized a conspiracy against the government, attracting indebted aristocrats, disaffected veterans, and other establishment opponents. Cicero exposed the conspiracy through a series of speeches to the Senate; Catiline fled Rome and joined an armed rebellion in Etruria. Gallic envoys revealed evidence of conspirators, allowing Cicero to arrest several participants and execute some.
- 62 BCE: Roman forces defeated Catiline's rebel army at the Battle of Pistoria, killing Catiline and ending the uprising.

### Caesar - Rise

- 61 BCE: Conducted successful campaigns in western Iberia as governor of Hispania Ulterior, acquiring wealth and military prestige.
- 60 BCE: Abandoned the opportunity to celebrate a triumph so that he could stand for the consulship, overcoming opposition from Cato the Younger.
- 60 BCE: Won election as consul and entered an informal political alliance with Pompey and Crassus (First Triumvirate).
- 59 BCE: Became consul alongside Bibulus and despite his opposition, passed agrarian reform for land distribution (settling Pompey's veterans), approval of Pompey's eastern conquests and stricter regulations against provincial corruption.
- 59 BCE: Pompey married Caesar's daughter Julia, strengthening their alliance.
- 59 BCE: Retaliated against Cicero's opposition by allowing Clodius into a plebeian family, enabling him to seek the tribunate.
- 59 BCE: Secured 5-year provincial of Illyricum, Cisalpine and Transalpine Gaul; oferring military opportunities and protection from prosecution.

### Caesar - The Gallic wars

- 58 BCE: The Helvetii (Celts) began migrating westward into Gaul, creating tensions with neighboring tribes and Roman interests, Caesar refused their passage and costructeed fortifications along the Rhône. After they took an alternate path through Gaul, his Gallic allies asked for support and Caesar pursued and defeated them (Arar, Bibracte); survivors had to return to their homeland, serving as a buffer against Germans.
- 58 BCE: Caesar confronted Ariovistus, a Germanic ruler that was expanding into Gaul. After negotiations failed, he defeated him at Vosges, forcing the survivors to retreat across the Rhine. Rather than withdrawing, Caesar stationed his legions in winter quarters within independent Gaul.
- 57-56 BCE: Defeated Belgic tribes in northern Gaul, and suppressed revolt by Veneti (maritime people of Brittany).
- 56 BCE: Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus renewed their political alliance at the Conference of Luca.
- 55 BCE: Caesar constructed a wooden bridge across the Rhine and led a brief expedition into Germania to send a message.
- 55 BCE: Caesar's first expedition to Britain was forced to withdraw due to local resistance, logistics and storm damage.
- 54 BCE: Caesar returned to Britain with a larger army, defeating rebels and securing agreements with several British rulers before withdrawing.
- 54 BCE: Julia died in childbirth, weakening his relationship with Pompey.
- 54–53 BCE: Gallic rebels destroyed a Roman force and besieged another; Caesar relieved them and launched campaigns against rebels, crossing the Rhine again to deter Germanic intervention.
- 52 BCE: Vercingetorix united several Gallic tribes in a major revolt, adopting scorched-earth tactics. Caesar captured Avaricum after a prolonged siege, but suffered a significant defeat at Gergovia.
- 52 BCE: Caesar besieged Vercingetorix at Alesia, constructing two rings of fortifications; Vercingetorix surrendered.

### Set up to civil war

- 58 BCE: Tribune Clodius forced Cicero into exile, introduced free grain distributions and annexed Cyprus, arranging for Cato the Younger to oversee it, removing him from Rome.
- 57 BCE: Cicero returned from exile, rival gangs associated with Clodius and Milo increasingly used violence.
- 55 BCE: Co-consuls Pompey and Crassus extended Caesar's command in Gaul and secured commands of Hispania (Pompey) and Syria (Crassus).
- 54 BCE: Crassus assembled a large army in Syria to invade the Parthian Empire.
- 53 BCE: Battle of Carrhae - Parthian cavalry defeated Crassus; Crassus and his son Publius were killed.
- 53 BCE: Crassus's and Julia's death ended the First Triumvirate and politically separated Caesar and Pompey.
- 52 BCE: Clodius was killed during a confrontation, triggering riots; his supporters burned the Senate house during his funeral.
- 52 BCE: Senate appointed Pompey as sole consul to restore order.
- ~52–50 BCE: Pompey aligned himself with Caesar's opponents who disputed over his command, potential prosecution, and right to consulship.
- 50 BCE: Caesar proposed relinquishing his military command if Pompey did likewise, but negotiations failed.

### Civil war

- January 49 BCE: Tribunes including Mark Antony opposed the senate and fled to Caesar's camp.
- 10 January 49 BCE: Caesar crossed the Rubicon with Legio XIII.
- January–March 49 BCE: Caesar encountered limited resistance and showed clemency. Pompey and many senators withdrew south.
- March 49 BCE: Pompey evacuated from Brundisium to Greece, escaping Caesar's siege and blockade.
- April 49 BCE: Caesar entered Rome and seized funds from the treasury to finance his army, despite tribune opposition.
- Summer 49 BCE: Caesar forced surrender of Pompey's legions in Hispania and some joined him istead.
- December 49 BCE: Caesar briefly assumed the dictatorship to organize elections that secured his consulship.
- January 48 BCE: Caesar crossed the Adriatic, surprising Pompey's forces. However, his remaining troops were blocked from immediately following.
- Spring 48 BCE: Mark Antony transported remaining reinforcements across the Adriatic despite the blockade.
- ~July 48 BCE: Battle of Dyrrhachium - Pompey broke through Caesar's fortifications and defeated his forces, but failed to pursue out of fear.
- 9 August 48 BCE: Battle of Pharsalus - Caesar decisively defeated Pompey's numerically superior army.
- August–September 48 BCE: Caesar pardoned several defeated opponents, including Brutus, continuing his policy of clemency.
- 28 September 48 BCE: Pompey was assassinated upon arriving in Egypt by agents of Ptolemy XIII's government, who hoped to gain Caesar's support; Caesar was disgusted.
- October 48 BCE: Caesar arrived in Alexandria and intervened in the dynastic struggle between Cleopatra VII and her brother Ptolemy XIII.
- 48–47 BCE: Alexandrian War - Ptolemy XIII's supporters fought Caesar in Alexandria, leading to urban fighting and naval engagements.
- ~March 47 BCE: Battle of the Nile - Caesar defeated Ptolemy XIII and, rather than annexing Egypt, restored Cleopatra VII under Roman protection.
- 47 BCE: Caesar defeated Pharnaces II of Pontus at Zela, ending his rebellion (veni, vidi, vici).
- 47 BCE: Caesar returned to Rome and removed Mark Antony from control of the city following unrest and his controversial behavior.
- 47 BCE: Caesar's veteran legions mutinied over unpaid rewards and delayed discharge. He persuaded them to resume service by promising eventual retirement.
- 6 April 46 BCE: Battle of Thapsus - Caesar decisively defeated remaining senatorial forces in North Africa. Cato the Younger committed suicide at Utica.
- 46 BCE: Caesar celebrated four triumphs in Rome (Gaul, Egypt, Pontus, and Africa). Vercingetorix was executed during the celebrations.
- ~46–44 BCE: Caesar's reforms: reducing the number of citizens receiving free grain, establishing overseas colonies for veterans and poorer citizens, extending citizenship to selected provincial communities, expanding the senate and admitting provincials into it, public works and urban improvements, and Julian calendar (365 days + leap year every 4 years).
- Autumn 46 BCE: Caesar launched a campaign against the sons of Pompey who had assembled a new army in Hispania.
- 17 March 45 BCE: Battle of Munda - Caesar narrowly defeated the Pompeian forces, ending organized military resistance to his rule.

### Caesar's dictatorship and assassination (46–44 BCE)

- 46 BCE: Cleopatra VII visited Rome with her son Caesarion, strengthening diplomatic relations between Rome and Egypt.
- 45 BCE: Caesar celebrated a triumph over his victory in Hispania, provoking criticism for celebrating the defeat of fellow Romans.
- ~45–44 BCE: Caesar intensified fears of monarchy by wearing triumphal regalia and receiving exceptional public recognition.
- 45 BCE: Caesar appointed loyal supporters as replacement consuls and increasingly controlled political institutions.
- Early 44 BCE: Caesar was appointed dictator for life.
- February 44 BCE: Mark Antony publicly offered Caesar a diadem, which Caesar rejected.
- Early 44 BCE: Caesar prepared a military expedition against Parthia.
- Early 44 BCE: A conspiracy of ~60 senators, including Brutus and Cassius formed to assassinate Caesar and prevent the establishment of monarchy.
- 15 March 44 BCE: Julius Caesar was assassinated during a Senate meeting; assasins proclaimed Rome liberated but it triggered a new political crisis.

### Octavian

- 15 March 44 BCE: Mark Antony (consul) emerged as the leading Caesarian politician. The conspirators failed to establish a stable replacement government.
- March 44 BCE: Caesar's will named his 18-year-old great-nephew Gaius Octavius as his heir, giving him Caesar's name and most of his fortune.
- March 44 BCE: Antony and the Senate reached a compromise granting amnesty to Caesar's assassins while preserving Caesar's legislation.
- March 44 BCE: Caesar's public funeral provoked unrest against his assassins, contributing to their departure from Rome.
- Spring 44 BCE: Octavius returned from Apollonia, accepted Caesar's inheritance and adoption, and began building support among Caesar's veterans.
- Summer–autumn 44 BCE: Rivalry intensified between Antony and Octavian over Caesar's political legacy and the loyalty of his veteran legions.
- September 44 BCE: Cicero began delivering his Philippics, a series of speeches attacking Antony and defending Republican institutions, increasingly supporting Octavian.
- 44 BCE: Antony secured legislation transferring the governorship of Cisalpine Gaul to himself, challenging its existing governor, Decimus Junius Brutus.
- November 44 BCE: Two legions transferred from Macedonia defected from Antony to Octavian, strengthening Octavian's military position.
- Late 44 BCE: Antony marched north to take control of Cisalpine Gaul from Decimus Brutus.
- January 43 BCE: The Senate granted Octavian military authority, allowing him to join the consuls gainst Antony.
- 14–21 April 43 BCE: Battles of Forum Gallorum and Mutina - consular armies defeat Antony and he retreats; both consuls die, leaving Octavian in command.
- Summer 43 BCE: Antony joined forces with Lepidus in Gaul, while Decimus Brutus was abandoned by his troops and subsequently executed.
- 19 August 43 BCE: Octavian marched on Rome with his army and secured election as consul.

### Second triumvirate

- November 43 BCE: Antony, Octavian and Lepidus formed the Second Triumvirate, a legally established government for five years.
- Late 43 BCE: Triumvirs revived proscriptions, killing opponents and confiscating their property to finance their armies; and taxed Italian peoples.
- 7 December 43 BCE: Cicero was executed during the proscriptions.
- 3 October 42 BCE: First Battle of Philippi - Antony captured Cassius's camp while Brutus's forces overran Octavian's camp. Cassius committed suicide.
- 23 October 42 BCE: Second Battle of Philippi - Antony and Octavian defeated Brutus, who committed suicide.
- Late 42 BCE: Antony took control of the eastern provinces, Octavian west, Lepidus increasingly marginalized.
- ~42–41 BCE: Octavian confiscated land across Italy to settle ~40,000 veterans, provoking unrest among dispossessed landowners and dissatisfied soldiers.
- ~42–39 BCE: Sextus Pompey (son of Pompey), controlled Sicily and disrupted grain supplies to Italy, causing unrest.
- 41 BCE: Antony met Cleopatra VII in Cilicia, forming a political and romantic alliance that secured Egyptian support for his eastern ambitions.
- 41–40 BCE: Antony's brother and wife rebelled against Octavian, exploiting resentment; Octavian defeated them.
- 40–38 BCE: Parthian forces invaded Roman Syria and Judea, but Antony's general defeated them killing the Parthian prince Pacorus.
- 40 BCE: Octavian seized control of Gaul and its legions, provoking Antony. Their soldiers resisted another civil war. Treaty of Brundisium reaffirmed the triumvirate, dividing territories between Octavian (west), Antony (east), and Lepidus (Africa). Antony married Octavian's sister Octavia.
- 39 BCE: Pact of Misenum recognized Sextus Pompey's control of Sicily, Sardinia, and Corsica in exchange for lifting his blockade and allowing political exiles to return.
- 38 BCE: Octavian married Livia Drusilla, whose descendants would become central to the Julio-Claudian dynasty.
- 38 BCE: Octavian renewed war against Sextus Pompey but suffered disastrous naval defeats near Sicily.
- 37 BCE: Treaty of Tarentum renewed the triumvirate for another five years; Antony provided ships for Octavian's campaign against Sextus in exchange for promised legions for his Parthian expedition, which Octavian failed to deliver.
- ~37 BCE: Antony reorganized Rome's eastern client kingdoms, appointing loyal rulers to strengthen Roman influence.
- 36 BCE: Agrippa defeated Sextus's fleet, securing Sicily for Octavian. Sextus escaped but was captured and executed in 35 BCE.
- 36 BCE: Lepidus attempted to claim Sicily, but his soldiers defected to Octavian, who stripped him of political power.
- 36 BCE: Antony invaded the Parthian kingdom of Media Atropatene through Armenia with a large army, but lost his siege equipment to enemy cavalry and was forced to retreat, suffering heavy casualties from attacks, hunger, and harsh weather.
- 35–33 BCE: Octavian campaigned in Illyricum, subduing rebellious communities and strengthening his military reputation.
- 34 BCE: Antony invaded Armenia as revenge for withdrawal of their support, and put it under Roman control.
- 34 BCE: Donations of Alexandria: Antony distributed royal titles and claims over east territories to their children, while Caesarion was proclaimed Caesar's legitimate son and co-ruler of Egypt, threatening Octavian's political legitimacy.
- ~34–32 BCE: Octavian intensified propaganda against Antony, portraying his relationship with Cleopatra and adoption of eastern royal customs as a betrayal of Roman traditions, while Antony accused Octavian of illegitimate rule.
- 33 BCE: The Second Triumvirate's renewed term expired, leaving Antony and Octavian openly competing.
- 32 BCE: Two consuls and numerous senators loyal to Antony fled Rome after Octavian confronted the Senate with armed supporters.
- 32 BCE: Octavian seized Antony's will from the Vestal Virgins and publicized his wish to be buried in Alexandria, to discredit him. Rome subsequently declared war on Cleopatra's Egypt rather than directly on Antony.
- 32–31 BCE: Antony and Cleopatra assembled their forces in western Greece, establishing a naval base in the Ambracian Gulf near Actium. Agrippa and Octavian disrupted their supply routes.
- 2 September 31 BCE: Battle of Actium: Octavian's fleet, commanded largely by Agrippa, defeated the forces of Antony and Cleopatra off western Greece. Antony and Cleopatra escaped to Egypt, while most of their remaining fleet surrendered.
- September 31 BCE: Antony's remaining land forces surrendered to Octavian.
- ~31–30 BCE: Octavian faced renewed unrest among veterans demanding promised land and rewards, temporarily returning to Italy to secure their loyalty.
- 30 BCE: Octavian invaded Egypt from the east and west, encountering limited resistance as Antony's remaining allies and soldiers increasingly defected.
- 1 August 30 BCE: Antony's remaining forces surrendered near Alexandria. Believing Cleopatra had died, Antony attempted suicide and died shortly afterward.
- August 30 BCE: Cleopatra VII died, probably by suicide, after Egypt fell to Octavian.
- 30 BCE: Octavian annexed Egypt, ending the Ptolemaic Kingdom. He pardoned some former enemies and eliminating threats (like Caesarion).
