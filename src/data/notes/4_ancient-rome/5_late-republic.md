# Late Roman republic

Rome's Mediterranean conquests brought enormous wealth, slaves, and opportunities for political advancement, but these benefits were distributed unevenly. Aristocrats established personal patronage networks abroad and competed for lucrative military commands and provincial appointments.

In Italy, wealthy landowners increasingly consolidated agricultural estates, often employing slaves. Small farmers faced pressures, and some migrated to cities for work. As fewer citizens met the property qualifications for recruitment, they were gradually lowered.

Tensions between the ruling elite and poorer citizens intensified: disputes over land distribution, military service, and political representation increasingly challenged the Senate's authority. Politicians began appealing directly to popular assemblies, leading to split of populares and optimates.

Recruitment of poorer volunteers contributed to the gradual development of longer-serving armies whose soldiers increasingly depended on commanders for rewards and post-service settlement.

Individual generals were increasingly given more authority, reflecting the Republic's increasing reliance on powerful individual commanders.

### Tiberius Gracchus

- 168 BCE: Born into a prominent Roman family (mother Cornelia was daughter of Scipio Africanus).
- 146 BCE: Began his military career serving under Scipio Aemilianus during the destruction of Carthage.
- 137 BCE: In Hispania negotiated a treaty to save a trapped Roman army; Senate rejected the agreement, damaging his political standing.
- 133 BCE: Elected tribune and proposed agrarian reforms to address land concentration: land cap and redistribution to poor.
- 133 BCE: Tribune Marcus Octavius vetoed the proposal; Tiberius secured his removal, challenging the traditional tribune protections.
- 133 BCE: Tiberius bypassed the Senate and secured passage of his agrarian law through the popular assembly.
- 133 BCE: A three-member land commission began surveying and redistributing public land.
- 133 BCE: King Attalus III of Pergamum died and bequeathed his kingdom to Rome; Tiberius proposed using the inheritance to finance his agrarian reforms.
- 133 BCE: Facing political prosecution after his term, Tiberius sought re-election, provoking accusations of personal rule.
- 133 BCE: Tiberius Gracchus and many supporters were killed by a group of senators during an electoral assembly.

### Gaius Gracchus

- 154 BCE: Born into a prominent Roman family; his mother Cornelia raised him.
- 133 BCE: Following the murder of Tiberius, Gaius inherited the political expectations and support networks.
- 126 BCE: Served as quaestor in Sardinia; repeated extensions of his commander's appointment kept him away from Rome.
- 124 BCE: Returned to Rome without authorization and won election as tribune.
- 123 BCE: Introduced a grain law to sell grain at a fixed, subsidized price, helping protect poor against high food prices.
- 123 BCE: Introduced legislation restricting capital punishment without popular authorization.
- 123 BCE: Revived his brother's agrarian reforms, restoring efforts for land redistribution.
- 123 BCE: Military reforms, including restrictions on recruiting under 17 and requiring the state to provide clothing.
- 123–122 BCE: Reformed the courts, transferring jury from senators to members of the equestrian order and weakening senatorial control over prosecutions.
- 123 BCE: Secured a second consecutive tribunate.
- 122 BCE: Proposed extending Roman citizenship to Latin communities and other Italian allies, but faced opposition from senators and population.
- 122 BCE: Promoted the establishment of new colonies, including in former Carthage, to reduce land pressure in Italy.
- 122 BCE: Introduced changes to provincial taxation.
- 122 BCE: Travelled to North Africa to oversee preparations for the colony; his political opponents gained influence during his absence.
- 122 BCE: Failed to secure election to a third tribunate.
- 121 BCE: Political tensions escalated after a supporter of Gaius killed an attendant of the consul Lucius Opimius during a confrontation.
- 121 BCE: The Senate issued the senatus consultum ultimum (emergency decree) urging the consuls to protect the Republic, which Opimius used to justify armed action against Gaius and his supporters.
- 121 BCE: Gaius Gracchus and Marcus Fulvius Flaccus died during the violent suppression of their movement; many supporters were subsequently executed.
- 121 BCE: Some Gracchan reforms survived their creators, although the agrarian programme was gradually weakened and the proposal to extend citizenship to Italian allies failed.

### Marius

- 157 BCE: Born near Arpinum in Latium, into a locally prominent family.
- 135–132 BCE: The First Servile War (Sicily); slaves rebelled against harsh treatment by wealthy landowners; suppressed by Roman.
- 134–133 BCE: Served under Scipio Aemilianus during the Numantine War, gaining military experience and recognition.
- 119 BCE: Elected tribune of the plebs; reforms restricting interference with voters.
- 115 BCE: Elected praetor, advancing his career despite his status as a novus homo.
- 115 BCE: Marcus Licinius Crassus born into a wealthy Roman aristocratic family.
- ~114–113 BCE: Governed Further Spain, conducting military operations against local resistance.
- ~110 BCE: Married Julia, a member of the prominent Julii Caesares family.
- 109 BCE: Accompanied Metellus to Numidia as his senior lieutenant during the Jugurthine War.
- 109 BCE: The Cimbri defeated a Roman army commanded by Marcus Junius Silanus in southern Gaul.
- 107 BCE: Won consulship by criticizing Metellus's slow campaign against Jugurtha and promising a decisive victory.
- 107 BCE: Recruited volunteers without traditional property qualifications, opening military service more widely to landless citizens.
- 107 BCE: The Tigurini, a group associated with the Helvetii, defeated a Roman army under Lucius Cassius Longinus.
- 106 BCE: Marius's quaestor Sulla negotiated with king of Mauretania to secure his betrayal.
- 106 BCE: Pompey the Great born into a prominent Italian family with a strong military and political background.
- 105 BCE: Jugurtha surrendered to Sulla, ending the war and beginning the rivalry between Marius and Sulla.
- 105 BCE: The Cimbri and their allies annihilated Roman armies commanded by Caepio and Gnaeus Mallius Maximus at Arausio due to disputes.
- 104–100 BCE: The Second Servile War (Sicily), due to disputes over the enslavement from Rome's allied communities; suppressed by Rome.
- ~104–103 BCE: The Cimbri moved into Hispania while the Teutones remained in Gaul, giving Rome time to rebuild its forces under Marius.
- ~104–100 BCE: Adopted the cohort as principal tactical unit, standardized equpiment, recruited poor, carrying their own equipment and intensifying training.
- 102 BCE: Defeated the Teutones and Ambrones at Aquae Sextiae, destroying a major invading force.
- 102 BCE: The Cimbri crossed the Alps into northern Italy, overcoming Roman attempts to block their advance.
- 101 BCE: With his consular colleague defeated the Cimbri at the Battle of Vercellae, ending the immediate threat.
- 100 BCE: Serving his sixth consulship, damaged his relationship with popular movement by suppressing political unrest.
- 100 BCE: Gaius Julius Caesar born into the ancient patrician Julii family, which had lost much of its former political prominence.

### Social war

- ~125–91 BCE: Italian allies increasingly demanded Roman citizenship as they contributed to Rome's military campaigns without enjoying equal rights.
- 91 BCE: Tribune Drusus proposed extending citizenship to Italian allies, but he was assasinated, triggering the war.
- 91 BCE: Several Italian peoples, particularly Marsi and Samnites, rebelled and established an independent confederation with its capital at Corfinium.
- 90 BCE: Rome suffered several military defeats; Gaius Marius returned to military command.
- 90–89 BCE: Rome passed laws extending citizenship to loyal allies and rebels willing to accept Roman authority, gradually undermining the rebellion.
- 89 BCE: Sulla achieved major victories against the southern rebels, while Pompeius Strabo captured the important stronghold of Asculum.
- ~89–87 BCE: The Social War ended with most Italian communities gaining Roman citizenship.

### Sulla

- 88 BCE: Mithridates VI of Pontus invaded Roman-controlled territories in Asia Minor and orchestrated the Asiatic Vespers.
- 88 BCE: A political struggle over command of the war against Mithridates led Sulla to march his legions on Rome, Marius fled to exile.
- 87 BCE: Sulla departed to fight Mithridates, while political conflict resumed in between the consuls Cinna and Gnaeus Octavius.
- 87 BCE: Cinna expelled from Rome and allied with returning Marius; their forces captured the city and carried out violent reprisals.
- ~87–82 BCE: Caesar married Cornelia, daughter of Cinna, and was nominated as Flamen Dialis (priest of Jupiter).
- 86 BCE: Marius began his seventh consulship alongside Cinna but died shortly afterward, leaving Cinna as the dominant figure.
- 86 BCE: Sulla captured and sacked Athens after a prolonged siege.
- 86 BCE: Sulla defeated the forces of Mithridates at the battles of Chaeronea and Orchomenus, securing Roman control of mainland Greece.
- 86–85 BCE: Rome dispatched a rival army under Flaccus to the east; following a mutiny, Fimbria assumed command and fought Mithridates independently.
- 85 BCE: Sulla concluded the Peace of Dardanus with Mithridates, requiring him to abandon his conquests while retaining kingdom of Pontus.
- 85–84 BCE: Sulla imposed heavy financial penalties on cities in Roman Asia.
- 84 BCE: Cinna killed during a military mutiny while preparing forces to confront Sulla.
- 83 BCE: Sulla returned to Italy with five legions to overthrow the Marian government, many commanders defected to his side.
- 83–82 BCE: Pompey, Crassus, and Metellus Pius supported Sulla, helping him defeat the Marian forces.
- 82 BCE: Sulla defeated the younger Gaius Marius at Sacriportus and besieged him at Praeneste, while his allies defeated other Marian armies across Italy.
- 82 BCE: Sulla defeated a Samnite-led army at the Battle of the Colline Gate, securing control of the capital; Crassues playing a decisive part in victory.
- 82 BCE: Sulla was appointed dictator with extraordinary powers and no fixed term limit.
- 82–81 BCE: Sulla introduced the proscriptions: lists of political enemies, property was confiscated, enriching his supporters.
- 82–81 BCE: Pompey defeated remaining Marian forces in Sicily and Africa, consolidating Sulla's control.
- 82–78 BCE: Crassus accumulated enormous wealth by purchasing confiscated property and investing in land, mining, and slaves.
- 81-80 BCE: Sulla expanded the Senate, restricted tribunal power, reorganized the cursus honorum, transferred jury control to senatros and settled his veterans on confiscated Italian land.
- 81 BCE: Pompey celebrated a triumph for his victories in Africa despite never having held a magistracy, bypassing traditions.
- 81 BCE: Sulla stripped Caesar of his priesthood and demanded that he divorce Cornelia, which Caesar refused, briefly going into hiding.
- ~80–79 BCE: Sulla voluntarily resigned his dictatorship and returned to ordinary political life, eventually retiring from public office.
- ~80s BCE: Cicero established himself as a prominent lawyer and orator, combining Roman legal practice with Greek philosophy and rhetoric.
- 80 BCE: Caesar served in army in Asia Minor and received the corona civica for bravery during the siege of Mytilene.
- 78 BCE: Sulla died.

### Pompey and Crassus

- 78–77 BCE: Marcus Aemilius Lepidus attempted to challenge established order but was defeated by forces loyal to the Senate, including Pompey.
- 77 BCE: Pompey received proconsular command in Hispania despite lacking qualifications, joining Metellus Pius against the Marian commander Quintus Sertorius.
- 77–72 BCE: Sertorius successfully resisted Roman armies in Hispania.
- ~75 BCE: Caesar was captured by pirates while travelling to Rhodes, later capturing and executing them.
- 72 BCE: Sertorius assassinated, Pompey ends major resistance in Hispania.
- 73 BCE: ~70 gladiators, led by Spartacus and Crixus, escaped from a gladiatorial school in Capua, beginning the Third Servile War.
- 73 BCE: Rebel army established a base near Vesuvius, attracting slaves and defeating several Roman forces sent to suppress them.
- 72 BCE: The rebel army divided, with Crixus remaining in southern Italy while Spartacus marched north. Crixus was defeated and killed by Roman forces.
- 72 BCE: Spartacus defeated Roman consular armies but then turned south for uncertain reasons.
- 72–71 BCE: Crassus received command against Spartacus and imposed strict military discipline.
- 71 BCE: Spartacus attempted to cross into Sicily but was abandoned by pirates. Crassus trapped his army and defeated them. Pompey intercepted and destroyed a separate group of fleeing rebels.
- 71 BCE: Crassus ordered approximately 6,000 captured rebels crucified along the Appian Way as a warning against future uprisings.
- 70 BCE: Crassus and Pompey became consuls together despite their rivalry.

### Third Mithridatic War

- 74 BCE: King Nicomedes IV of Bithynia died and bequeathed his kingdom to Rome, provoking conflict with Mithridates VI of Pontus.
- 73–72 BCE: Mithridates invaded Bithynia, beginning the Third Mithridatic War. Roman commander Lucullus defeated his forces and invaded Pontus.
- 71–70 BCE: Lucullus conquered much of Pontus, forcing Mithridates to flee to Armenia, where he sought protection from his son-in-law, King Tigranes II.
- 69 BCE: Lucullus invaded Armenia and defeated Tigranes II at the Battle of Tigranocerta.
- 69 BCE: Caesar served as quaestor in Hispania Ulterior. At his aunt Julia's funeral, he publicly displayed images of her husband Marius.
- 68–67 BCE: Lucullus's campaigns stalled amid difficult conditions, weakening his political support in Rome.
- 67 BCE: The Lex Gabinia granted Pompey military authority across the Mediterranean to combat piracy; his naval campaign defeated and resettled pirates.
- 66 BCE: The Lex Manilia transferred command of the war against Mithridates to Pompey.
- 66 BCE: Pompey defeated Mithridates in Pontus; Tigranes II submitted to Rome, Armenia became a client kingdom.
- 66–65 BCE: Pompey campaigned in the Caucasus against local kingdoms, extending Roman military influence beyond Anatolia.
- 65 BCE: As aedile, Caesar financed lavish public games and building projects, gaining popularity while accumulating debts.
- 64 BCE: Pompey annexed remaining Seleucid territories in Syria.
- 63 BCE: Pompey intervened in a Hasmonean dynastic conflict in Judea, captured Jerusalem, and established Roman dominance over the kingdom.
- 63 BCE: Mithridates VI died after losing his remaining political and military support, bringing the Third Mithridatic War to an end.
- 63 BCE: Caesar was elected Pontifex Maximus, Rome's chief priest.
- 62 BCE: Caesar served as praetor, aligning himself with Pompey's while maintaining financial ties with Crassus.
- 61 BCE: Pompey returned to Rome and celebrated a magnificent triumph, having expanded Roman influence and doubled tax revenues.

### Caesar on the rise

- 64 BCE: Cicero defeated Catiline in the consular elections, benefiting from senatorial opposition to Catiline's proposals for debt relief.
- 63 BCE: Catiline lost another consular election and began organizing a conspiracy against the government, attracting indebted aristocrats, disaffected veterans, and other opponents of the political establishment.
- 63 BCE: Cicero exposed the conspiracy through a series of speeches to the Senate. Catiline fled Rome and joined an armed rebellion in Etruria.
- 63 BCE: Gallic envoys from the Allobroges revealed evidence implicating leading conspirators, allowing Cicero to arrest several participants and execute five of them.
- 62 BCE: Roman forces defeated Catiline's rebel army at the Battle of Pistoria, killing Catiline and ending the uprising.
- 61 BCE: As governor of Hispania Ulterior, Caesar conducted successful military campaigns in western Iberia, acquiring wealth and military prestige.
- 60 BCE: Caesar abandoned his opportunity to celebrate a triumph so that he could stand for the consulship, overcoming opposition from Cato the Younger.
- 60 BCE: Caesar won election as consul and established an informal political alliance with Pompey and Crassus (First Triumvirate).
- 59 BCE: Caesar became consul alongside Bibulus and despite his opposition, passed agrarian reform for land distribution (settling Pompey's veterans), approval of Pompey's eastern conquests and stricter regulations against provincial corruption.
- 59 BCE: Caesar arranged Pompey's marriage to his daughter Julia.
- 59 BCE: Caesar retaliated against Cicero's opposition by allowing Clodius into a plebeian family, enabling him to seek the tribunate.
- 59 BCE: Caesar secured 5-year provincial of Illyricum, Cisalpine and Transalpine Gaul; this offered military opportunities and protection from prosecution.

### The Gallic wars

- 58 BCE: The Helvetii (Celts) began migrating westward into Gaul, creating tensions with neighboring tribes and Roman interests, Caesar refused their passage and costructeed fortifications along the Rhône.
- 58 BCE: After receiving requests from Gallic allies, Caesar pursued Helvetii into independent Gaul, defeating part of them (Battle of the Arar).
- 58 BCE: Caesar defeated the Helvetii at the Battle of Bibracte; survivors had to return to their homeland, serving as a buffer against Germans.
- 58 BCE: Caesar confronted Ariovistus, a Germanic ruler that was expanding into Gaul; negotiations failed.
- 58 BCE: Caesar defeated Ariovistus (Battle of Vosges), forcing the survivors to retreat across the Rhine.
- 58–57 BCE: Rather than withdrawing, Caesar stationed his legions in winter quarters within independent Gaul.
- 57 BCE: Caesar defeated Belgic tribes in northern Gaul, including the Nervii, who nearly overwhelmed his army.
- 56 BCE: Caesar suppressed a revolt by the Veneti, a maritime people of Brittany, defeating their fleet.
- 56 BCE: Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus renewed their political alliance at the Conference of Luca.
- 55 BCE: Caesar constructed a wooden bridge across the Rhine and led a brief expedition into Germania to send a message.
- 55 BCE: First expedition to Britain, but forced to withdraw due to local resistance, logistics and storm damage.
- 54 BCE: Return to Britain with a larger army, defeating rebels, and securing agreements with several British rulers before withdrawing.
- 54 BCE: Caesar's daughter Julia, wife of Pompey, died in childbirth, weakening their personal relationship.
- 54–53 BCE: Gallic rebels destroyed a Roman force and besieged another; Caesar relieved the besieged troops.
- 53 BCE: Caesar launched punitive campaigns against rebels and crossed the Rhine again to deter Germanic intervention.
- 52 BCE: Vercingetorix united several Gallic tribes in a major revolt, adopting scorched-earth tactics.
- 52 BCE: Caesar captured Avaricum after a prolonged siege, massacring much of its population, but subsequently suffered a significant defeat at Gergovia.
- 52 BCE: Caesar besieged Vercingetorix at Alesia, constructing two rings of fortifications to contain the defenders and repel a Gallic relief army. Vercingetorix surrendered.
