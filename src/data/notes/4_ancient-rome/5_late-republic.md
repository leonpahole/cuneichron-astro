# Late Roman republic

Rome's Mediterranean conquests brought enormous wealth, slaves, and opportunities for political advancement, but these benefits were distributed unevenly. Aristocrats established personal patronage networks abroad and competed for lucrative military commands and provincial appointments.

In Italy, wealthy landowners increasingly consolidated agricultural estates, often employing slaves. Small farmers faced pressures, and some migrated to cities for work. As fewer citizens met the property qualifications for recruitment, they were gradually lowered.

Tensions between the ruling elite and poorer citizens intensified: disputes over land distribution, military service, and political representation increasingly challenged the Senate's authority. Politicians began appealing directly to popular assemblies, leading to split of populares and optimates.

### Tiberius Gracchus

- 168 BCE: Born into a prominent Roman family (mother Cornelia was daughter of Scipio Africanus).
- 146 BCE: Began his military career serving under Scipio Aemilianus during the destruction of Carthage.
- 137 BCE: In Hispania negotiated a treaty to save a trapped Roman army; Senate rejected the agreement, damaging his political standing.
- 133 BCE: Elected tribune and proposed agrarian reforms to address land concentration: land cap and redistribution to poor.
- 133 BCE: Tribune Marcus Octavius vetoed the proposal; Tiberius secured his removal, challenging the traditional tribune protections.
- 133 BCE: Tiberius bypassed the Senate and secured passage of his agrarian law through the popular assembly.
- 133 BCE: A three-member land commission began surveying and redistributing public land.
- 133 BCE: King Attalus III of Pergamum died and bequeathed his kingdom to Rome; Tiberius proposed using the inheritance to finance his agrarian reforms.
- 133 BCE: Facing political prosecution after his term, Tiberius sought re-election, provoking accusations of personal rule.
- 133 BCE: Tiberius Gracchus and many supporters were killed by a group of senators during an electoral assembly.

### Gaius Gracchus

- 154 BCE: Born into a prominent Roman family; his mother Cornelia raised him.
- 133 BCE: Following the murder of Tiberius, Gaius inherited the political expectations and support networks.
- 126 BCE: Served as quaestor in Sardinia; repeated extensions of his commander's appointment kept him away from Rome.
- 124 BCE: Returned to Rome without authorization and won election as tribune.
- 123 BCE: Introduced a grain law to sell grain at a fixed, subsidized price, helping protect poor against high food prices.
- 123 BCE: Introduced legislation restricting capital punishment without popular authorization.
- 123 BCE: Revived his brother's agrarian reforms, restoring efforts for land redistribution.
- 123 BCE: Military reforms, including restrictions on recruiting under 17 and requiring the state to provide clothing.
- 123–122 BCE: Reformed the courts, transferring jury from senators to members of the equestrian order and weakening senatorial control over prosecutions.
- 123 BCE: Secured a second consecutive tribunate.
- 122 BCE: Proposed extending Roman citizenship to Latin communities and other Italian allies, but faced opposition from senators and population.
- 122 BCE: Promoted the establishment of new colonies, including in former Carthage, to reduce land pressure in Italy.
- 122 BCE: Introduced changes to provincial taxation.
- 122 BCE: Travelled to North Africa to oversee preparations for the colony; his political opponents gained influence during his absence.
- 122 BCE: Failed to secure election to a third tribunate.
- 121 BCE: Political tensions escalated after a supporter of Gaius killed an attendant of the consul Lucius Opimius during a confrontation.
- 121 BCE: The Senate issued the senatus consultum ultimum (emergency decree) urging the consuls to protect the Republic, which Opimius used to justify armed action against Gaius and his supporters.
- 121 BCE: Gaius Gracchus and Marcus Fulvius Flaccus died during the violent suppression of their movement; many supporters were subsequently executed.
- 121 BCE: Some Gracchan reforms survived their creators, although the agrarian programme was gradually weakened and the proposal to extend citizenship to Italian allies failed.
