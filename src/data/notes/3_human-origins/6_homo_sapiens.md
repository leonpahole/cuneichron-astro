# Homo sapiens

Homo sapiens evolved in Africa and eventually became the only surviving human species. Our success resulted not from a single evolutionary breakthrough but from a combination of biological adaptability, cooperation, language, technology and culture. As populations spread into new environments, natural selection and cultural adaptation produced substantial variation while continued migration and interbreeding kept humans as a single species.

## Timeline

### Origins in Africa

- ~300–200 thousand BCE: Evolved in Africa from earlier Homo populations, mix of modern and archaic features.
- ~200 thousand BCE: The common maternal ancestor of all living humans (mitochondrial Eve) lives in Africa.
- ~100–50 thousand BCE: Increasingly complex symbolic behavior, long-distance exchange, specialized tools, personal ornaments and hunting strategies.
- ~100–50 thousand BCE: "Cultural evolution", allowing response to environmental changes faster than genetic evolution alone.

### Expansion across the world

- ~120–90 thousand BCE: Early populations reach eastern Mediterranean, including Skhul and Qafzeh (Israel), but it doesn't lead to the permanent global expansion.
- ~70–60 thousand BCE: Major expansion out of Africa begins, probably through northeastern Africa and the Arabian Peninsula.
- ~65–50 thousand BCE: South and Southeast Asia, eventually the islands of Wallacea.
- ~65–50 thousand BCE: Australia and New Guinea (repeated sea crossings) with sophisticated planning and seafaring.
- ~50–40 thousand BCE: Much of Europe, encountering and interbreeding with Neanderthals.
- ~45–40 thousand BCE: East Asia, including China and Siberia.
- ~25–15 thousand BCE: Enters Americas, probably via Beringia (exact timing and routes remain debated).
- ~15–12 thousand BCE: Spread through much of Americas, from the Arctic to South America.

### From foraging to farming

- ~15–10 thousand BCE: Some hunter-gatherers begin proto-farming; encouraging or protecting useful wild plants and returning to productive areas.
- ~10 thousand BCE: Agriculture develops independently in several regions, including the Fertile Crescent, China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica and the Andes.
- ~10–9 thousand BCE: Communities in the Fertile Crescent increasingly cultivate wheat, barley, legumes and other plants and begin domesticating animals.
- ~9600–8200 BCE: Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Anatolia contains monumental communal structures built by hunter-gatherers.
- ~9500–8000 BCE: Wadi Faynan in Jordan contains early sedentary communities and evidence of plant use and experimentation with cultivation during the transition to agriculture.
- ~8500–7000 BCE: Nevalı Çori in southeastern Anatolia develops a settled community with monumental buildings, domesticated crops and evidence of organized ritual activity.
- ~7000 BCE: Farming spreads and intensifies across much of Eurasia, eventually supporting larger permanent settlements and increasingly complex societies.

### Culture and society

- ~100–50 thousand BCE: Humans increasingly use symbolic culture - ornaments, pigments, etc. to communicate identity, relationship and shared beliefs.
- ~100–50 thousand BCE: Humans develop increasingly sophisticated clothing and sewing technologies.
- ~50-10 thousand BCE: Complex language allows humans to coordinate large groups, transmit knowledge and maintain shared traditions.
- ~10 thousand BCE: Organized religion and shared ritual become increasingly important forms of social cohesion.
- ~10 thousand BCE: Warfare becomes increasingly visible archaeologically as populations become more sedentary and resources, territory and social inequalities become more important.
- ~3400–3000 BCE: The earliest known writing develops in Mesopotamia, initially for administrative purposes.
